The word this is named for

prompt · wrought

\ ˈprɒm(p)t-ˌrɔːt \ adj.
  1. 1.

    Made by asking. Shaped in language rather than in metal, wood or stone.

  2. 2.

    Of a made thing whose maker cannot fully say which parts are theirs.

  3. 3.

    A lexicon, published in issues. One coined word each time, with its definition, its roots, and the case for keeping it.

There is a craft here. It has no vocabulary.

The tooling arrived before the language did. We have all spent two years doing something daily that we cannot name — and reaching, every time, for a borrowed word that nearly fits.

That gap closes eventually. Somebody names these things. It may as well be done carefully, one word at a time, with the roots dug up and checked before anything is struck.

The lexicon so far

Nº 005

promptwright n. \ ˈprɒm(p)t.raɪt \ Naming

A person skilled in the craft of writing and refining instructions for generative AI systems to produce a reliable, intended result; a professional prompt-maker.

Etymology

From prompt (n., 14th c., via Latin promptus, ‘brought forth, ready’) + -wright (Old English wryhta, ‘worker, maker’), the agentive suffix of playwright, shipwright and wheelwright. The suffix is the older half by several centuries, and it does the carrying: say the word aloud and it lands like a trade, not a title.

In use

“We hired a promptwright to turn the marketing team’s vague briefs into reliable, repeatable AI output.”

The case

A craft gets a vocabulary, and this one has been working without a name for its worker — prompt engineer arrived pre-embarrassed, two words wide, the second borrowed from people who build bridges. When an age needs a title for its newest job and takes the suffix from the oldest ones, it is telling you something: the work is older than the machine.

grainsense n. \ ˈgreɪn.sɛns \ Knowing

A practised feel for a particular machine’s tendencies — where it drifts, what it over-does, which phrasings it obeys; the promptwright’s knowledge of the material.

Etymology

From grain (Latin granum, ‘seed’; the direction of fibres in wood) + sense. After the carpenter’s first rule: work with the grain.

In use

“Grainsense is why her prompts transfer badly — they’re tuned to one model’s wood.”

The case

The thesis of the whole volume in a single word: this is a material craft, and the material has a grain.

overwright v. \ əʊ.vəˈraɪt \ Making

To keep refining an instruction past the point of improvement; to polish a working prompt until it breaks.

Etymology

Back-formed from overwrought (‘overworked; agitated’) on the maker-suffix -wright — and deliberately a homophone of computing’s overwrite, because over-editing destroys the version that worked. Three words fold into one, and every maker knows the fault the moment they hear it.

In use

“It ran fine three versions ago. Then I overwrighted it.”

The case

The prompt was working — that is what makes this one dangerous. Over-instructing is harder to catch than its opposite because it wears the mask of diligence: under-instructing feels like laziness and you notice it, while adding one more clause feels like care, and nobody flinches at care. Naming the fault turns it into craft — the sound of a maker recognising the moment to put the tool down and step back from the bench.

promptbook n. \ ˈprɒm(p)t.bʊk \ Keeping

A personal, accumulated collection of proven instructions, kept and refined the way a cook keeps recipes; a promptwright’s working grimoire.

Etymology

After playbook and spellbook — but chiefly a revival. The promptbook was a real object of the Victorian theatre: the stage manager’s marked copy, from which the play was actually run.

In use

“Hang on — that one’s in my promptbook.”

The case

Everyone finds the phrasing that works and then loses it. What is missing is not effort but an object — a place to keep the instructions that earned their keep. A restored word carries an authority a freshly minted one has still to earn.

misask n. & v. \ mɪsˈɑːsk \ Asking

The gap between what one asked for and what one meant; to instruct a machine accurately in the wrong direction.

Etymology

From mis- (Old English, “wrongly”) + ask (Old English āscian, “to seek”). Pure Anglo-Saxon; no seam.

In use

“The output wasn’t wrong — my question was. A straight misask.”

The case

It relocates blame from the machine to the phrasing, which is the founding insight of the entire craft — and it is short enough to be said in meetings.

From the desk

The -wright suffix is one of the oldest job descriptions in English. It comes from Old English wyrhta, a worker or maker, and it survives now almost entirely in trades that have gone: the wheelwright, the shipwright, the millwright, the cartwright. Only the playwright is still at work.

Which is exactly what makes it available. The suffix is unclaimed. And wrought — the old past participle of work — carries something that made does not: the sense of a thing worked into shape by hand, with effort, and with the marks of the working still on it.

That seemed like the right word for what this is.

Emma Darling, editor
